    Sizing Advice & Generator Type Selection:

    For loads requiring under 4,000 Watts with sensitive electronics (laptops, TVs, smartphones), an Inverter Generator is strongly recommended for clean pure sine wave power (THD < 3%). For loads exceeding 5,000 Watts with whole-home AC and water pumps, consider a Dual-Fuel Portable Generator or permanently wired Standby Generator paired with a manual or automatic transfer switch.

    What This Tool Does & Problem It Solves

    The Generator Size Calculator accurately computes the electrical wattage and kVA required to power your critical household or job-site equipment during a grid outage. Choosing a generator that is too small leads to chronic overload trips, engine stalling, and dangerous voltage sags that can burn out refrigerator and air conditioning compressors. Conversely, choosing an unnecessarily oversized generator wastes fuel, produces excessive noise, and risks engine cylinder glazing from running under insufficient mechanical load. This calculator applies engineering-grade motor surge staggering logic, accounts for elevation derating, and factors in safe continuous operating margins.

    Mathematical Formulas Used

    In residential and job-site operation, appliances do not start simultaneously. A well-designed electrical setup powers appliances in sequence: the largest motor is started first, followed by smaller motors and resistive lighting loads. Thus, total peak surge is mathematically formulated as:

    • $$\text{Total Running Watts} = \sum (\text{Unit Running Watts} \times \text{Quantity})$$
    • $$\text{Max Single Surge Delta} = \max_{i} (\text{Surge Watts}_i - \text{Running Watts}_i)$$
    • $$\text{Peak Starting Surge Watts} = \text{Total Running Watts} + \text{Max Single Surge Delta}$$
    • $$\text{Required Continuous kW} = \frac{\text{Total Running Watts} \times (1 + \text{Safety Margin}) \times (1 + \text{Altitude Derate})}{1000}$$
    • $$\text{Commercial kVA Rating} = \frac{\text{Recommended Generator kW}}{\text{Power Factor (0.8)}}$$

    Technical Specifications & Mechanics

    • Harmonic Distortion Consideration: Conventional generators produce 10% to 20% THD; sensitive electronic boards can overheat. Inverter generators synthesize pure sine waves (<3% THD).
    • Motor Surge Delta Principle: Prevents oversizing by staggering startup sequence instead of summing non-coincident starting currents.
    • Altitude Derating Compensation: Automatically adds capacity when operating above 2,000 ft or in ambient heat above 40°C.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Can I run my generator continuously at 100% capacity?

    No. Manufacturers design generators to deliver their rated continuous watts under normal conditions, but running them at 100% load continuously produces severe thermal stress, accelerates engine wear, increases fuel consumption, and risks tripping the circuit breaker. Always size your generator so your continuous load represents no more than 75% to 80% of its rated capacity.

    Will a generator damage sensitive electronics like laptops and televisions?

    Conventional portable generators with open frames can produce dirty power with harmonic distortion exceeding 15%, causing overheating in delicate digital power supplies. Use an Inverter Generator with clean pure sine wave power (THD < 3%) or connect an online double-conversion UPS between a conventional generator and sensitive electronic equipment.

    How many watts does it take to run a central AC unit?

    A standard 3-Ton central air conditioner requires roughly 3,000 to 3,500 running watts, but demands a massive starting surge between 7,000 and 10,000 watts. Installing an aftermarket Soft Starter (such as an EasyStart) reduces compressor startup surge by up to 60%–70%, allowing a much smaller generator (e.g. 5 kW to 7.5 kW) to start and run the unit effortlessly.

    What is the difference between kW and kVA on generator spec sheets?

    kW (kilowatts) measures real, working electrical power, while kVA (kilo-volt-amperes) measures apparent power. Because alternating current electric motors introduce inductive reactance, current and voltage fall slightly out of phase. In commercial generator ratings with a standard 0.8 Power Factor (PF), a 7.5 kW generator is rated at $7.5 / 0.8 = 9.4\text{ kVA}$.
